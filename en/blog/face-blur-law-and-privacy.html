"Can I get in trouble for posting a photo with someone else's face in it?" It's a common hesitation. This article covers how the law treats facial data, the four highest-risk scenarios, and a minimum-operations checklist you can just follow.

⚠️ General information, not legal advice. Consult a qualified lawyer for specific situations.

1. Why facial data is regulated separately

Unlike a name or an ID number, a face is public, unchangeable, and capturable from a distance. A leaked password can be reset; a leaked face cannot. Because of that irreversibility, facial data is widely classified as sensitive personal information, held to a stricter standard than ordinary personal data.

Under regimes like the GDPR (Art. 9) and China's PIPL, processing biometric data generally requires explicit, separate consent plus a specific purpose and necessity. So "I took the photo, therefore I can publish it" does not hold.

2. Four high-risk scenarios

1. Street photography and bystanders

A bystander's likeness is their personal data even in a public place. Whether publishing is lawful depends on purpose, commercial use and harm — but publishing a clearly identifiable stranger's face leaves you in a weak position if they object.

Do: blur or crop non-subject faces before publishing. Cheapest, safest option.

2. Minors

Children's data attracts a higher standard of protection. Even with a parent present, get consent before posting photos containing other people's children.

Do: blur their faces, or shoot from behind / in profile only.

3. CCTV and access-control stills

Posting surveillance frames ("catch this thief", "help me find them") is risky: it can infringe likeness and personal-data rights, and if you identify the wrong person it can amount to defamation. The correct route is handing footage to the police, not publishing it yourself.

4. Work contexts — customers, patients, colleagues

Marketing photos, medical case images, before/after renovation shots: faces appearing in commercial promotion normally need signed permission. Unauthorised commercial use is the highest-risk case.

3. Common misconceptions

BeliefReality
"Public place = free to publish"❌ Public space is not a waiver of likeness or data rights
"I'm not making money, so it's fine"❌ Non-commercial use can still infringe
"A small friends-only post is harmless"⚠️ Limited reach reduces liability, but doesn't make it lawful
"Once blurred, it's totally safe"⚠️ Blurring cuts risk sharply but clothing, scene and build can still identify someone
"If they never notice, it's fine"❌ Being pursued and being unlawful are different things

4. A checklist you can actually follow

  1. Blur or crop every non-subject person. Don't deliberate case by case — default to blurring. It costs seconds and removes the judgement call.
  2. Cover the whole face. Eyes-only is ineffective. Cover brows, eyes, nose, mouth, jaw, and pad outward 10–20% to include hair and ears.
  3. Extra care with minors. Default to blurring, or shoot from behind.
  4. Hand suspected-crime footage to police. Don't self-publish "exposés".
  5. Get permission first for commercial use. Blurring doesn't replace consent — if the person is the subject, a blurred subject means the photo has no purpose, which tells you consent was needed all along.

5. The irony: blurring tools can leak the very faces you're protecting

Many online face-blurring tools require you to upload the photo to their server first — while the thing you're protecting is exactly the faces in that photo.

Worse, such services aggregate enormous volumes of facial imagery. One breach affects far more people than your single post ever would.

How to check a tool in 30 seconds

  1. Open the tool and press F12 → Network panel
  2. Clear the log, then process an image
  3. Look for a large POST going out

A large POST → your image was uploaded. Static assets only → it stayed local.

Even more direct: disconnect your network after the page loads, then process an image. If it still works, the computation is local.
